Mechanisms

The mechanisms

Liveness, and the role of time

  • The commit rule counts references; it does not refer to time.
  • Liveness is reduced to two facts about the DAG, from some round on:
    • every reliable validator has a block in each round;
    • each reliable block references every reliable block of the round below.
  • These follow from view convergence and the pacemaker's rules.
Uses: view convergence and the pacemaker's rules (slide 64).
Gives: the support's precondition live (slide 56).
  • The commit rule counts references and does not refer to time, so the liveness argument is built in layers, and only the lower layers refer to time.
  • Read the diagram from top to bottom: each arrow is labelled with what the layer below receives. The network gives view convergence after GST, the global stabilisation time after which every block reaches every reliable validator within the delay Δ.
  • The pacemaker turns view convergence into two facts about the DAG, from some round on: every reliable validator has a block in each round, and each reliable block references every reliable block of the round below. The reliable validators are a set of at least quorum size that follows the pacemaker.
  • These two facts are the support's precondition live (slide 56), and Law 2 turns live into commits. For Mysticeti it is proved in Lean that the two facts give a reliable leader a certificate, and hence a direct commit.

The pacemaker's rules

  • Advance: a validator holding blocks of n − f creators at round r moves past round r.
  • Catch up: after GST, a validator that holds a reliable block of round r enters round r within proc, the time to process and build a block.
  • Wait: a validator spends at least timeout(r) in round r.
  • Reference: a validator's block references every block of the previous round that it holds.
  • The pacemaker decides when a validator leaves a round; four rules govern it.
  • The diagram follows one validator's round counter from top to bottom; each arrow is labelled with the rule that governs it.
  • Catch up (top arrow): after GST, a validator that holds a reliable block of round r enters round r within proc, the time to process a block and build its own. Wait (the box “in round r”): it spends at least timeout(r) in round r.
  • Advance (middle arrow): a validator that holds blocks of n − f distinct creators at round r moves past round r. Reference: the block it then builds references every round-r block it holds.
  • Advance, with view convergence, gives every reliable validator a block in every round. Reference, with Wait and a large enough timeout, gives that each reliable block references every reliable block below. The next slide shows how large the timeout must be.

Drift collapses in one round

  • Validators' clocks may drift apart before GST.
  • After GST, catching up brings every reliable validator within Δ + proc of the others in one round, whatever the spread before.
  • A timeout of 2Δ + proc therefore suffices; it does not depend on the spread before GST.
  • Before GST the validators' round entry times may drift arbitrarily far apart; this slide shows that, at any round that every reliable validator enters after GST, the spread is at most Δ + proc.
  • In the chart the horizontal axis is the round and the vertical axis is each validator's entry time minus the earliest entry at that round. With GST = 12, Δ = 2, proc = 1, v3 enters round 0 ten units late, before GST; from round 1 it is 3 = Δ + proc late. v0 is Byzantine, and its line coincides with v1's.
  • The mechanism: after GST, the earliest validator's block reaches the last validator within Δ, and Catch up makes the last validator enter that round within proc. This is proved in Lean for any spread before GST.
  • Consequently a timeout of 2Δ + proc suffices for each reliable block to reference every reliable block below, and the bound has no term for the spread before GST. In the example the timeout is 5 = 2·2 + 1, and the decision of the slots is checked on the concrete DAG with no hypothesis on the spread.
  • The statement that waiting alone, without Catch up, does not reduce drift is informal: it has no Lean statement and is not proved.

The reactive schedule, and local liveness

  • A validator waits only until it holds the leader's block, then votes; the timeout is a fallback.
  • The vote round then takes at most drift + delivery + processing. When that sum is below the timeout, no timeout fires.
  • Every reliable validator decides from the blocks it holds, by an explicit time.
  • The reactive schedule shortens rounds: a validator waits only until it holds the leader's block, then votes, and the full timeout is a fallback.
  • In the table, validator v builds its round-r block at time v + 6r, so each validator spends 6 time units in every round. The chart plots this constant 6 against the timeout line at 9 = 2Δ + proc, with Δ = 2 and proc = 5.
  • Proved in Lean: when every reliable block of the leader's round reaches every reliable validator within δ of its creation, the round above is built within D + δ + proc of round entry, where D is the drift. When this sum is below the timeout, no timeout fires.
  • Local liveness: after GST, every reliable validator decides the slot from the blocks it holds itself, by an explicit time. This strengthens the earlier statement, which reads the whole DAG.
  • The reactive schedule gives up the guarantee that each reliable block references every reliable block of the round below. Inclusion of correct blocks, defined on the next slide, is still proved, along each validator's own chain once that validator leads a slot.

Chain quality

  • Coverage: the history of every committed block contains, at every round below it, blocks of at least |Correct| − f correct validators. No timing assumption is needed.
  • Inclusion: a correct validator's block is committed once a later slot it leads commits.
  • Coverage holds for every rule except Bluestreak. Inclusion holds for the seven rules with SelfParent and NoEquiv.

Equivocation and exposure

  • Safety requires no restriction on equivocation; bounded storage does.
  • A creator is exposed in a block's history when the history holds two of its blocks in one round.
  • Rule: never reference a block whose creator is exposed in your own history.
  • This alone bounds a history, but with a constant that grows like f^f: one history can hold several chains of one creator (2^e chains with e = 2 in the figure).
Uses: no property of the rule; only which blocks a validator accepts.
Gives: a bound on what a correct validator stores (next slide).

The novelty budget

  • Accept a block only if its history adds at most τ blocks you do not hold; reference only blocks you have accepted.
  • The rule is independent of a block's creator.
  • Storage then grows linearly: with C correct validators, a correct validator holds at most C·((1 + fτ)·r + 1 + f) blocks by round r. At C = 3, f = 1, τ = 3, round 9: 114 blocks.
  • The novelty budget makes storage grow linearly in the round, by limiting what one acceptance may add to a validator's store.
  • The novelty of a block, for a validator, is the set of blocks in its history that the validator does not hold. The rule: accept a block only if its novelty has at most τ blocks, and reference only accepted blocks. The rule does not depend on the block's creator, so a validator can enforce it without knowing which validators are Byzantine.
  • Proved in Lean, from round 0 and without timing assumptions: with C correct validators, a correct validator holds at most C·((1 + fτ)·r + 1 + f) blocks by round r.
  • The chart evaluates the bound at C = 3, f = 1, τ = 3: 12r + 6 blocks, from 6 at round 0 to 114 at round 9.
  • Also proved: once every correct block is delivered from some round on, a correct block's novelty is at most f·κ + 1, where κ bounds the novelty of an accepted Byzantine block.
  • The bound still grows with r; garbage collection, on the next slides, makes it constant.

The cut

  • Each validator keeps a horizon G and discards every block below it. Validators do not agree on G.
  • Keep the blocks of rounds ≥ G and renumber round G as round 0, with no references. The result is again a valid DAG.
  • Slot d + k of the full DAG and slot k of the cut DAG have the same verdict.
Uses: Banded, Agree, and Law 1 of the support. Gives: the same verdicts and liveness after the cut, and constant storage (next slide).
  • Garbage collection discards old blocks; the cut is the operation on the DAG that describes the result.
  • Each validator keeps a horizon G and discards every block below it; different validators may hold different horizons.
  • Step 1: in the left figure, G = 2; the faded blocks of rounds 0 and 1 are discarded.
  • Step 2: the right figure is the cut DAG. Rounds 2–5 are renumbered 0–3, and the round-2 blocks, such as block 8, become round-0 blocks with no references; the result is again a valid DAG.
  • Proved in Lean: when G is at most the round of slot d, slot d + k of the full DAG and slot k of the cut DAG have the same verdict, and validators with different horizons agree. In the figure d = 1: block 11 is committed by slot 1 before the cut and by slot 0 after it, checked on this DAG.
  • The proofs use Banded, Agree and Law 1, and give the same verdicts and liveness after the cut. The next slide bounds storage.

Storage is constant

With the horizon at most Λ rounds below the current round, a correct validator keeps at most C·(Λ + 1) + C·f + Λ·C·f·κ blocks, whatever the round. Here κ bounds the number of new blocks that accepting one Byzantine block adds to the store. At C = 3, f = 1, κ = 3, Λ = 5: 66 blocks.

  • With garbage collection, the bound on storage no longer depends on the current round.

Joining from an attested base

  • A new validator adopts a round-G block when blocks of at least f + 1 distinct creators at a later round t reach it.
  • One of those creators is correct, so every base block is attested by a correct validator. No signatures are needed.
  • It then copies a correct peer's blocks above G. Its verdicts equal those of any validator with the full history.
  • A new validator cannot obtain the discarded history; this slide shows how it chooses a starting layer at round G without trusting any single peer.
  • It adopts a round-G block when blocks of at least f + 1 distinct creators at a later round t reach it; the adopted blocks form the attested base. In the figure G = 1, t = 4, and the round-4 blocks 14, 15, 16 have three creators, 3 ≥ f + 1 = 2.
  • Step 1: among f + 1 creators at least one is correct, so every base block lies in the history of a correct validator's block, with no signatures required. Here the base is {5, 6, 7}, and every base block is reached from all three attesters; this is checked on the DAG.
  • Step 2: the validator then copies a correct peer's blocks above G, here 8, 9, 10, and holds blocks 5–10.
  • Step 2: proved in Lean: after the network synchronises, and when t is at least two rounds above the peer's latest round, the new validator's verdicts never conflict with those of any validator holding the full history.

Crash recovery: the message

  • A validator v₁ crashed after its block B₁ and returns at round r. It cannot create a block: a valid block needs its own previous block one round below.
  • It sends one message: B₁, r, a donor validator v₂, and fresh identifiers.
  • Every receiver computes a filled block for v₁ at each missed round: the donor's references and payload at that round, plus a reference to v₁'s previous block.
Uses: Banded (through Persist), Agree, SkipsUnsupported, Law 1. Gives: v₁ producing again, with every earlier verdict unchanged.
  • Crash recovery: a validator v₁ that missed rounds cannot create a block, because a valid block needs its creator's previous block one round below. In the left figure, v₁ = v3 stops after block 3, which is B₁.
  • Step 1: on its return at round r, it sends one message: B₁, r, a donor validator v₂, and fresh identifiers for the new blocks. In the right figure the donor is v1, with blocks 5, 9, 13, 17.
  • Step 2: every receiver computes a filled block for v₁ at each missed round: the donor's references and payload at that round, plus a reference to v₁'s previous block. These are the dashed blocks 21–24; block 21 references {3, 0, 1, 2}, that is, block 3 together with the references of donor block 5.

What the fill guarantees

  1. The filled DAG is valid, and every old block is unchanged.
  2. v₁ has a block at every missed round, so it can produce again.
  3. A slot led by v₁ in the missed rounds is skipped at once, given a quorum one round above (the prompt skip). No later view can commit it.
  4. Every verdict reached before the fill agrees with every verdict after it.
  • This slide lists what the fill guarantees; each item is proved in Lean.
  • Step 1: the filled DAG is valid, and every old block is unchanged, so every store, view and certificate built on the old DAG reads the same data.
  • Step 2: v₁ has a block at every missed round, the shaded blocks 21–24 at rounds 1–4, which is the production fact that liveness requires.
  • Step 3: the prompt skip. A slot led by v₁ in the missed rounds has only filled blocks as candidates, and no old block references them; given a quorum of old blocks one round above, the slot is skipped at once (SkipsUnsupported), and with Agree and Banded no later view commits it. It is proved for Mysticeti, Odontoceti, Hybrid, Hydrozoan and Bluestreak.
  • Step 3: in this example the schedule places no slot led by v3 in rounds 1–4; the prompt skip is shown on slide 80.
  • Step 4: every verdict reached before the fill agrees with every verdict reached after it, from Persist and Agree.

Jumping rounds

  • A validator's own blocks form a chain, so the donor's whole line is fixed by its top block.
  • The fill depends only on B₁, the donor's top block B₂, and the fresh identifiers. The message carries these and the two validators v₁, v₂.
  • A receiver that holds B₂ holds the donor's whole line. After the one message, v₁ has a block at every round up to B₂'s round, so it can produce at the next.
  • One message suffices for any number of missed rounds, because the donor's line of blocks is determined by its top block.
  • In the diagram the top row is the donor v1's line and the bottom row is v3's: B₁ = 3 and the filled blocks 21–24. Dashed arrows show which donor block each filled block copies its references from; green arrows are self-references.
  • A validator's own blocks form a chain, so from B₂ = 17 the previous own blocks 13, 9, 5 follow. Proved in Lean: the fill depends only on B₁, B₂ and the fresh identifiers, so the message carries these and v₁, v₂.
  • A receiver that holds B₂ holds the donor's whole line, since a view contains the history of each of its blocks. After the one message, v₁ has a block at every round up to round 4, the round of B₂, and can produce at round 5.
  • The statement that the message has constant size is informal: the fresh identifiers are modelled as a supply, and no size bound is proved.

Limits of the fill

  • With the horizon Λ rounds below the current round, one message recovers from an outage of at most Λ rounds.
  • After a longer outage, B₁ has been discarded.
  • No old block references a filled block, so v₁ is not covered in the rounds it missed.
  • The storage rule must be re-checked; with a donor line whose blocks all reach B₁, and v₁ not equivocating, it is one reachability check per round.
  • If the donor line is taken from blocks every receiver already holds, nothing else needs to be sent.
  • This slide states the limits of the fill.
  • Step 1: with the horizon Λ rounds below the current round, one message recovers from an outage of at most Λ rounds; proved in Lean, B₁ is retained exactly when the outage is at most Λ. In the figure r = 10, Λ = 5, rounds 5–10 are retained, and B₁ at round 5 is reached.
  • Step 2: after a 6-round outage, B₁ at round 4 has been discarded, and v₁ has no block in the cut DAG; the next slide treats this case.
  • Step 3: no old block references a filled block, so if v₁ is counted as reliable, the property that every reliable block references every reliable block of the round below fails at the missed rounds. With v₁ excluded, that property is unchanged.
  • Step 3: the storage rule must be re-checked for the filled blocks. Proved: if each donor block reaches B₁ and v₁ never equivocated, the filled DAG satisfies the rule, so the check is one reachability test per round.
  • Step 3: if the donor line is one that every receiver holds, every copied reference lies in the history of every block two rounds above it, so nothing beyond the message is sent; the self-reference is to v₁'s own block.

Re-genesis: recovering after a long outage

  • If all of v₁'s blocks are below the horizon, its chain of own blocks cannot be continued.
  • Each validator adds a round-0 block for v₁, with no references, to its cut DAG. This needs no exception to the self-parent rule.
  • Procedure: (1) join from the attested base to read; (2) re-genesis to write; (3) one fill message from the genesis block to the current round.
  • While a quorum still produces blocks, at most f validators can be in this state at once.
Uses: Banded, Agree, Law 1, and the attested base (slide 73). Gives: a validator back in production after any outage.
  • Re-genesis recovers a validator v₁ all of whose blocks are below the horizon. Proved in Lean: a validator with no round-0 block in a DAG has no block in it at all, since every block's chain of own blocks descends to round 0.
