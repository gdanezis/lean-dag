Every mechanism is one relation

Each mechanism relates the DAG it reads to the DAG it writes: the same blocks from a settling round on, renumbered by G rounds, with the same creators and payloads, and the same references strictly above the settling round.

  • A cut is such a relation; so are a fill and a re-genesis (with G = 0).
  • A sequence of them is again one: the composite keeps the same blocks from the later settling round on.
Uses: Banded, Agree, Law 1.
Gives: safety and liveness for any combination of cuts, fills and re-geneses (next slide).
  • This slide shows that a cut, a fill and a re-genesis are instances of one relation between the DAG a mechanism reads and the DAG it writes, and that a sequence of them is again an instance.
  • The relation has an offset G and a settling round. From the settling round on, the written DAG holds the same blocks at rounds lowered by G, with the same creators and payloads, and with the same references strictly above the settling round; below the settling round the relation says nothing.
  • The rows of the figure are drawn at D's rounds 0–20. In the top row, D lacks validator v₁'s blocks at rounds 6–10.
  • Step 1: a fill with r = 10 adds those blocks. It has offset 0 and settles at round r + 1 = 11; a re-genesis is also a relation with G = 0.
  • Step 2: a cut at G = 15 drops rounds 0–14 and renumbers rounds 15–20 as 0–5. It settles at round 15.
  • Step 3: the composite of the fill and the cut is again one relation; it settles at max(r + 1, G) = 15, and the shaded rounds 15–20 hold the same blocks in all three rows. That a finite sequence of such relations is one relation is proved in Lean.

Safe and live across any stack

From Banded, Agree and Law 1: across any stack, verdicts above the settling round are preserved, views agree, and the liveness precondition is preserved.
The cut, fill and re-genesis are each built once, and serve ten of the eleven rules.

The theorem has three conclusions: a verdict at a slot above the settling round is the same before and after the stack, at the renumbered slot; any view of the result agrees with the original view on those verdicts; and the liveness precondition of the support carries over to the renumbered slots.

Leaders chosen from the committed prefix

  • A fixed schedule may continue to name a crashed leader.
  • A configuration fixes the leaders of each round and an interval.
  • It decides up to its anchor (the first committed slot more than one interval past its start).
  • It outputs only up to its boundary.
  • The next configuration is computed from the anchor's history and the verdicts just output.
Uses: Agree (safety); Law 2 and Indirect (liveness).
Gives: a schedule every validator computes the same way.
  • Adaptive leaders replace a fixed leader schedule by a sequence of configurations, each computed from what has already been committed.
  • The motivation, that a fixed schedule may continue to name a crashed leader, is an informal argument and is not proved.
  • A configuration fixes the leaders of each round and an interval, the number of rounds before the next reconfiguration is due. In the figure each row is one configuration, with one leader per round and interval one, so a slot is a round.

  • Step 3: configuration 1 is computed from the anchor's history and the verdicts just output; it decides slot 3 again as 15 and outputs [ ], and configuration 2 outputs [15].

Safe for every anchored score

  • Two validators that start from the same configuration agree on every configuration and every verdict.
  • The theorem assumes no timing, fairness or window, and holds for every score that reads the view only through the anchor's history.
  • For liveness the score must continue to assign reliable validators runs of consecutive slots.
  • Two validators that start from the same configuration agree on every later configuration, anchor and verdict, for every anchored update rule; this is proved in Lean.
  • An update rule is anchored when it returns the same configuration on any two views of one DAG, given the same anchor and the same verdicts. A score that reads the view only through the anchor's causal history is anchored.
  • The top row is validator a on view V, with configurations C₀, C₁, C₂ linked by the update. Step 1: validator b on view V′ starts from the same C₀.
  • Step 2: the vertical links state the theorem segment by segment: equal anchors and verdicts for C₀, and C₁ = C₁′, C₂ = C₂′ with equal anchors and verdicts, for every configuration both validators have reached.
  • The only property of the base rule the theorem uses is Agree; it assumes no timing, fairness or window.
  • Liveness is proved under further conditions on the score: it keeps the number of slots per round and the interval, and the configurations it produces continue to give reliable validators runs of consecutive slots. What a score may read

  • A score that reads only the anchor's history is unaffected by crash recovery.
  • Under garbage collection the score must read verdicts relative to the configuration.
  • A score that names an absolute slot number gives different answers before and after a cut.
  • Crash recovery and garbage collection each restrict what a score may read.
  • Crash recovery, by a fill or a re-genesis, only adds blocks. A score that reads only the anchor's history returns the same configuration before and after; this is proved in Lean and checked for a concrete score.
  • Garbage collection cuts the DAG at a horizon G, and a joiner numbers its slots from the cut. The score must then be horizon-stable: on views that agree above G, the joiner's configuration is the network's with the rounds below G dropped. A score that only relabels leaders is horizon-stable at every cut.
  • In the figure the cut is at G = 1. The top row is the network, with slots numbered from round 0; the bottom row is the joiner, whose slots 1 and 2 are the network's slots 2 and 3, with the same verdicts.
  • Step 1: the score shown swaps two leaders when slot 1 committed block 5. The network reads its slot 1, sees commit 5 and swaps; the joiner reads its own slot 1, sees no commit and keeps the leaders. Checked on a concrete DAG, this score is not horizon-stable at G = 1.
  • A score that reads verdicts must therefore read them relative to the configuration it closes. The next mechanism uses the same anchors to change the number of leaders per round.

The adaptive leader count

More leaders per round lower latency, but one slow leader delays every later slot. At each anchor:

  1. count the directly committed slots in the anchor's history;
  2. healthy if num · expected ≤ den · observed;
  3. healthy: one more leader; unhealthy: 2^backoff fewer, between 1 and maxLeaders; the backoff resets when healthy and grows otherwise.
Uses: Agree, CommitsCandidate, CommitsDirect (the count reads direct commits).
Gives: a leader count every validator computes the same way.
  • The adaptive leader count changes the number of leaders per round at each anchor, by additive increase and multiplicative decrease (AIMD).
  • The motivation, that more leaders lower latency and that one slow leader delays every later slot, is an informal argument and is not proved.
  • observed is the number of slots in the window of interval + 1 rounds up to the anchor's round that are directly committed in the anchor's history. expected is the number of slots in the rounds of that window old enough to have been decided; at one width m it is (interval − waveLength + 1)·m. The window is healthy if num · expected ≤ den · observed.
  • A healthy window adds one leader and resets the backoff to 0; an unhealthy window removes 2^backoff leaders and increases the backoff by one. The count is kept between 1 and maxLeaders.
  • The DAG figure: interval 4, one leader per round, anchor 21 at round 5. In the anchor's history only the leaders 5 and 10 are directly committed, so observed = 2 = expected, the window is healthy, and the next configuration has two leaders per round. This is checked on the concrete DAG.
  • Step 1: the chart follows five windows, healthy, healthy, unhealthy, unhealthy, healthy. The count goes 1, 2, 3, 2, 1, 2 with backoff 0, 0, 0, 1, 2 before each window; maxLeaders = 4 is not reached. The sequence is checked by computation in Lean.
  • The rule uses Agree, CommitsCandidate and CommitsDirect; CommitsDirect makes every slot counted as directly committed a committed slot.

Checkpoints

  • Correct online validators sign the checkpoint their deterministic state machine reaches after each commit in their own view, and witness certificates for what they signed.
  • A checkpoint certificate is at least q signatures on one proposal; a finality certificate is at least q witnesses of one.
  • Two checkpoint certificates at one epoch and height agree.
  • A correctly led slot, with production and certification, is finalised.
  • At an epoch change, the highest validated checkpoint becomes the next epoch's genesis.
Uses: Agree, CommitsCandidate, Law 2.
Gives: finalised application state, and its recovery across epochs.
  • Checkpoints turn the committed sequence into certified application state, and carry that state from one epoch to the next.
  • Read the figure from left to right. A correct online validator applies each commit in its own view to a deterministic state machine and signs the checkpoint it reaches; at least q signatures on one proposal form a checkpoint certificate. Each validator then witnesses the certificate for what it signed, and at least q witnesses form a finality certificate.
  • Safety: two checkpoint certificates at one epoch and height have the same content. Their quorums share a reliable signer, and that signer signs one state per height. This holds within one epoch and is proved in Lean.
  • Liveness: a slot with a correct leader, under the production and certification conditions, is finalised; this is also proved.
  • Step 1: at an epoch change, certificates of epoch e are broadcast, and the highest validated checkpoint becomes the genesis of epoch e + 1. The broadcast is assumed, not modelled (slide 89).
  • The construction uses Agree, CommitsCandidate and Law 2, and is instantiated for Hybrid and for the adaptive-leader runs.

Red Snapper: a fast path for owned objects

  • Validators state a stance on each object version inside their ordinary blocks; certificates for transactions, skips and unlocks are read from the DAG.
  • At n ≥ 3f + 1, uncontested transactions are decided from the DAG (fast quorum).
  • Conflicts are resolved at a committed anchor.
  • At n ≥ 5f + 1, a validator may change its vote on 2f + 1 opposing votes, coordinated by a coin.
  • Among any n − f votes on two sides, the larger side always has at least 2f + 1 exactly when n ≥ 5f + 1. The 5f + 1 certificate lemmas themselves need only n ≥ 3f + 1.
Uses: only the committed anchor sequence of the underlying consensus.
Gives: commits of owned-object transactions without waiting for consensus, except under conflict.
  • Red Snapper is a fast path for transactions on owned objects, run inside the ordinary DAG blocks; it uses only the committed anchor sequence of the underlying consensus.
  • Each block states a stance on each object version: an ACK of one transaction or ⊥. A certificate is a block whose references hold enough stances: n − f ACKs for a transaction certificate, 2f + 1 ⊥ votes for a skip or unlock certificate.
  • Left figure: n = 4, validator 0 is Byzantine (shaded) and has no blocks after round 0. Block 4 carries and ACKs the transaction, blocks 7, 8 and 9 ACK it, and each round-3 block 10, 11, 12 is a certificate over 3 ACKs (q = 3). The three certificates form a fast quorum, and the transaction is final with no anchor yet; this is checked on the concrete DAG.
  • Step 1, right figure: two conflicting transactions on one object. Every correct block declares ⊥, each correct round-3 block is an unlock certificate, and the committed anchor 12 releases the object and drops both transactions.
  • At n ≥ 5f + 1 a validator may change its vote on 2f + 1 opposing votes, coordinated by a coin. It is proved that among any n − f votes the larger side reaches 2f + 1 exactly when n ≥ 5f + 1; the certificate safety lemmas of that protocol are proved at n ≥ 3f + 1.
  • The result: owned-object transactions commit without waiting for consensus, except under conflict.
